The Buyer's Brief: The Half-Page That Saves a Sourcing Round

Most bad sourcing starts before any supplier is contacted. It starts with a brief that leaves the important constraints out. What to write down, and what to leave out.

When a sourcing round goes sideways, the blame usually lands on the supplier. Nine times out of ten, the brief was the problem. Suppliers answer the questions you ask. If you do not ask the right ones, you will not get useful answers, and you will burn weeks comparing bids that cannot be compared.

A brief is a contract with yourself

Think of the brief as the specification your future self will defend against. Every vague word in it comes back as a negotiation point later. "Good quality" invites ten different interpretations. "Soon" invites ten different schedules. The supplier is not trying to mislead you. They are trying to give you a price, and they will price the ambiguity in a way that protects them.

The rule. If two suppliers would read your brief and picture two different products, the brief is not ready. Fix it before you send it.

What belongs on the half-page

You do not need a forty-page document for a straightforward buy. You need one page that locks the constraints that actually drive cost and risk. Here is the working list.

  • The standard. Name the norm or grade you are buying to, by its number. Not "structural steel". The grade and the standard.
  • Dimensions and tolerances. Exact size, and the tolerance band you can actually live with. Tighter tolerance means more cost, so only ask for what you need.
  • Quantity and unit. The number, and the unit it is measured in. Meters, tonnes, pieces. These are not interchangeable.
  • Destination and delivery window. Where the material lands and by when. Include whether the date is a hard deadline or a target.
  • Incoterms preference. Who carries freight and risk at each stage. This changes price more than the base rate does.
  • What is non-negotiable. State the constraints that cannot bend. Everything else is open, and you should say so.

What to leave out

A brief that says everything says nothing. The common failure is including your full internal story: why you are buying, your budget range, your urgent rationale. None of that improves the bid. It only gives the supplier room to anchor higher or hedge more.

Honest limitation. There is a balance. Some buyers deliberately reveal a budget to get suppliers into a workable band and avoid wasting everyone's time. It is a valid tactic. The point is to decide consciously whether to reveal it, not to leak it by accident.

The comparison problem

The second job of a good brief is making bids comparable. If each supplier quotes on different terms, you are comparing apples to shipping containers. Lock the variables that let you read every quote against the same frame: same standard, same quantity, same incoterm, same destination, same validity period for the price.

The decision frame
VariableLock it or you cannot compare
Standard / gradeYes. A grade is the unit of comparison.
QuantityYes. Volume changes per-unit price.
IncotermYes. Freight moves the real cost.
Validity periodYes. A price is only a price for so long.
Lead timeYes. Schedule is part of the offer.

Lock the frame, then read every quote against it.

Practical limitations

A strong brief removes most avoidable friction. It does not remove all of it. Some suppliers will still answer partially, or quote on their own standard, or pass the question to a sub-agent who was never briefed. Expect one clarifying round even on a clean brief, and budget for it in the timeline.

And a brief cannot fix a market problem. If the material is in short supply or the logistics lane is congested, the best written brief in the world will not conjure stock. What it does is make every conversation faster and every comparison fairer, which is most of the job.
